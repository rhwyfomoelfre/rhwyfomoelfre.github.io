<div class="shell content-shell"><article class="prose"><h2>Overview</h2><p>The ability to exercise is determined by the ability to transport fuel and oxygen to and waste products from the cells. In the healthy human, this process is limited by the cardiovascular system's performance and not by that of the respiratory system. The maximum amount of oxygen which can be transported per minute is termed the VO2 max. This is important in determining performance because it cannot be exceeded, but equally important is the maximum sustainable amount of work that can be performed.</p><p>The sustainability of exercise, at least in the short to medium term, is determined by whether the energy to perform the work is mainly supplied by aerobic metabolism, when oxygen is plentiful, or anaerobic metabolism. This is not an either/or situation: anaerobic metabolism supplements aerobic metabolism at the cellular level even at relatively low workloads. At these workloads, the products of anaerobic metabolism are further metabolised by the liver and remain at a constant low level. This is demonstrated by lactate being found in the blood of a person at rest, with the lactate remaining at a constant low level.</p><p>Lactic acid causes the blood to become more acidic, and that acidity affects the performance of all the body's organs. In this context, we are mainly concerned with the effect that lactate acidosis has on muscle performance. At low levels of work, the lactate concentration may initially rise but will then remain constant. These workloads can be sustained for a long period of time.</p><p>When the products of anaerobic metabolism, primarily lactate, begin to increase at a constant workload, it is only a matter of time until that workload can no longer be sustained. The threshold at which this increase occurs is referred to as the anaerobic threshold and occurs at approximately 60% of VO2 max. CPET, or cardio-pulmonary exercise testing, can infer these thresholds by measuring oxygen consumption and carbon dioxide production at various workloads. The most common way of doing this is with a static cycle with increasing resistance and a combined gas-content and volume analyser. Other, less accurate ways of inferring these thresholds also exist, using heart rate and running speed and correlating these with the work required to run at a certain pace.</p><figure class="training-figure"><img src="https://www.moelfrerowing.org.uk/sites/moelfrerowing.co.uk/files/inline-images/VO2.png" alt="VO2 max plotted against age"><figcaption>VO2 max varies with age and is one measure of aerobic capacity.</figcaption></figure><p>As the normal lungs cause the blood entering the left side of the heart to be fully saturated with oxygen, when exercising, the only way to supply the muscles with more oxygen is through the cardiovascular system pumping more blood and the exercising muscle extracting more oxygen from the blood passing through it. The cardiovascular system transports more oxygen by the heart beating more frequently and by increasing the volume of blood pumped per beat, or stroke volume. Both mechanisms are limited: heart rate by age, hence the equation for maximum heart rate of 220 minus age in years, and stroke volume by anatomy. Training can increase possible stroke volume up to its anatomical and physiological maximum. This explains the observation of slower heart rates in trained athletes: fewer beats are required to pump the same amount of blood if each beat pumps more blood. Changes in the exercising muscle, its blood supply, and the efficiency of the muscle also contribute to the positive effect of training on performance.</p><p>The anaerobic threshold need not be exceeded in order to perform effectively in an event lasting more than a few minutes. In practical terms, knowing your heart rate at the anaerobic threshold can help you understand how much work can be undertaken sustainably.</p><h2>Application to rowing</h2><p>Being a better rower is obviously about improving technique, but the importance of training the cardiovascular system cannot be overstated. The most time-efficient way to do this is through High Intensity Interval Training, or HIIT. Many different modes of exercise are suitable for HIIT, including rowing ergometers and spinning bikes. Fartlek is arguably an early type of HIIT. HIIT involves several repetitions of high-intensity exercise separated by medium-intensity exercise for recovery, with a suitable warm-up and warm-down, and many different regimes are promoted. The HIIT principle has been taken to the extreme with Super High Intensity Interval Training, where the load on the bike or other equipment is greater than the maximum normally achievable and the duration is very short. Two or three HIIT sessions a week have been shown to improve cardiovascular fitness in as little as four to six weeks, but it is important that other types of exercise are used in the training regime as well.</p><p>We have found that it is more efficient in terms of boat speed to increase the effort in the drive as much as possible while keeping a long stroke. The commonly held belief that making a boat go faster requires increasing the stroke rate may work in narrower, finer boats, but is flawed when applied to the heavier Celtics. Fast Celtic crews will be stroking at a faster rate, but the increase in stroke rate will initially occur naturally as an increase in boat speed shortens the time taken for the drive. Additional speed is progressively more difficult to gain as speed increases, and focusing on stroking more quickly invariably compromises the drive and shortens the recovery phase. A good analogy is felt on an ergo by increasing the damper setting to maximum: it is very difficult to maintain a high stroke rate, and increasing the stroke rate by accelerating the recovery does little to increase boat speed while appreciably increasing the workload. This leads to the rower exceeding their anaerobic threshold with little benefit in terms of boat speed. A fitter crew, with a higher anaerobic threshold, will be able to maintain technique and power in the drive at a faster stroke rate. That is why a younger crew with the potential for a much higher anaerobic threshold should always beat an older crew.</p></article><aside class="sidebar"><h2>Training</h2><a href="{{ '/training/' | relative_url }}">Training overview</a><a href="{{ '/training/stroke-guide/' | relative_url }}">Stroke guide</a></aside></div>
